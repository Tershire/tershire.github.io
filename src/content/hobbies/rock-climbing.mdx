Bouldering indoors keeps me sharp during the week; sport climbing outdoors is where I actually feel alive. Currently projecting V7 in the gym and working on leading 5.11c outside.

## How I Got Into It

A labmate dragged me to a climbing gym in my first year of grad school with the pitch that it was "like solving puzzles with your body." That description turned out to be accurate enough that I went back the next day.

Three years later I'm at the gym four times a week and spending most of my free weekends driving to crags.

## Indoor vs. Outdoor

They're almost different sports.

**Indoor bouldering** is where I do most of my training. The grades are consistent, the holds are clean, you can work a single problem for an hour, and failing doesn't hurt. It's pure problem-solving — read the sequence, test the moves, link them together.

**Outdoor sport climbing** has terrain, rock texture, weather, and consequences. The grades are inconsistent and route-setter ego doesn't exist. A 5.11a at one crag can feel like 5.10a or 5.12a at another. This uncertainty is part of the appeal.

## Current Projects

- **Outdoor**: Leading 5.11c at a local crag. The crux is a sequential move at the third bolt with no good rest before it.

## The Mental Side

The most useful thing climbing has taught me: the difference between a move being physically impossible and being psychologically blocked. Most cruxes are the latter. Learning to recognize that distinction, and then act anyway, transfers well to research.
