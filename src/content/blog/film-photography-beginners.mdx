I work with cameras professionally — depth cameras, event cameras, thermal cameras. I understand exactly how a CMOS sensor works, what noise characteristics look like, and why rolling shutter matters.

And yet, when I go outside to take pictures for myself, I reach for a camera that's older than I am.

## The Obvious Question

"Why would you shoot film? Digital is strictly better."

This is almost always the response. And from a purely technical standpoint, it's hard to argue. Modern digital cameras have better dynamic range, less noise, higher resolution, and instant feedback. You can shoot 1,000 frames for free.

But that framing misses the point entirely.

## What Film Does Differently

Film is not a worse version of digital. It's a fundamentally different tool that produces a fundamentally different process.

**You have 36 frames.** Not 36 shots before you change a card — 36 shots, period, before you develop. That constraint forces you to think before you shoot. You look at a scene, you wait for the right moment, you think about exposure. Every frame costs something, so every frame gets attention.

**The feedback loop is broken.** With digital, you shoot and immediately check the screen. With film, you shoot and wait days or weeks. This sounds like a downside. In practice, it teaches you to trust your instincts rather than chasing optimization. You can't chimp the shot, so you stop worrying about it and move on.

**The tonality is different.** This one is genuinely aesthetic and subjective. Kodak Portra's skin tones, Fuji Velvia's saturation, Ilford HP5's grain — these have a character that's difficult to replicate in post-processing, despite what presets claim.

## My Current Setup

I shoot on two cameras:

- **Pentax K1000** — fully mechanical, 100% reliable, built in 1976. No batteries required. It's the camera I learned on and I'll probably shoot it for the rest of my life.
- **Contax T2** — for when I want to carry something small. Expensive and fragile, but the Zeiss lens is worth every compromise.

For film, I mostly reach for:

- **Kodak Portra 400** — versatile, beautiful skin tones, forgiving overexposure
- **Ilford HP5+** — my black and white workhorse, pushes beautifully to 1600
- **CineStill 800T** — for city lights at night, the halation effect is gorgeous

## Developing at Home

About a year ago I started developing my own black and white film. It's surprisingly simple: you mix chemicals, pour them into a light-tight tank, agitate for 20 minutes, and rinse.

The first roll I developed myself felt like a small miracle. The images emerging on the strip — images I had composed and exposed weeks earlier — felt genuinely earned.

Color is harder (C-41 process is more temperature-sensitive), so I still send that out. But B&W at home is now a ritual I look forward to.

## The Unexpected Benefit

Here's something I didn't anticipate: film photography made me a better engineer.

Working within constraints forces creative problem-solving. Slowing down to think before acting is a habit that transfers. The willingness to accept uncertainty and commit anyway is a skill.

There's also something to be said for pursuing things that can't be optimized indefinitely. Software can always be faster. Papers can always be more rigorous. But a photograph taken with a 50-year-old camera, developed in a bathroom, is just... done. And that's enough.
